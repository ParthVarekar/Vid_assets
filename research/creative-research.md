# Creative research — the landscape this film enters

Researched 13 November 2026.

## Method and limits

This research is built from studio case studies, practitioner write-ups and published effectiveness research that I loaded and read. **I did not watch the films themselves.** The browser available in this session could not render video (its pane was hidden and screenshots timed out), and I cannot hear audio in any case. So every observation below about a film is the studio's or a critic's account of it, not my own viewing. Two search tools also failed partway through (one rate-limited, one misconfigured), which narrowed the set of sources. Treat this as a sound reading of how the category describes its own best practice, not as a frame-by-frame study.

## Findings and what we do about them

### 1. The throughline device is proven, and not original by itself

A single element that connects every scene appears in at least two of the references (Kavaliero's dot, Microsoft's ribbon), and "one-line animation" is a whole genre on Behance. So a continuous line is a reliable structure, not a fresh idea. What makes it ours has to come from two places: the line must be **an object the brand already owns**, and it must **change state for a reason**.

- Ours is drawn by the brand's own quill, and it is the idea itself: hand-drawn while it is only an idea, precise once it is engineered. The moment it snaps from sketch to geometry is "Creativity, Engineered" shown rather than said.
- Guidance: the line is never decoration. In every scene it must become the main subject (the window's outline, the workflow's path, the growth curve), or it is cut.

### 2. Da Vinci territory

I found no technology advertising built on Leonardo's notebooks in the sources I could reach; that is an absence of evidence in a limited search, not proof the territory is empty. The obvious risks are known ones: the Vitruvian Man, parchment-and-sepia pastiche, flying machines, mirror writing as a gimmick. Guidance: take the **method** (a notebook where ideas are worked out in line, with figure numbers and marginal notes), not the iconography. No Renaissance imagery appears in the film.

### 3. Kinetic typography

- One idea per beat; four to seven words on screen at once; the payoff word bigger, slower and held.
- Holds carry the meaning. Minimum settle for a headline: about 2.3–3.4 seconds (70–100 frames at 30fps). Single words: at least 1 second.
- One family, two weights, plus mono for labels. No outlines, glows or bounces.
- Entrances: masked rise with ease-out, 12–18 frames, narrow stagger. Exits designed, not faded by default.
- Nothing fully static: every hold rides a slow drift.

### 4. Agency promos: what to avoid

The category default, by the references' own description of what they were avoiding: a feature or services list, a narrator explaining, stock footage of people at laptops, a logo that only appears at the end. Guidance: capabilities appear as things being built on screen, labelled like notebook figures, never as a bullet list.

### 5. Sound

- Design a small vocabulary (arrive, draw, lock, tick, travel, land) and reuse it so the film sounds like one product.
- Tune tonal effects to the score's key.
- Short impacts for contact moments; low end reserved for the two or three hero beats.
- Write picture timings in beats; let the motion peak on the hit, starting about one frame early.
- Use a gap before the final hit so the ending lands as new.
- Deliver around −14 LUFS integrated with true peak at or below −1 dBTP, the common target across YouTube and social platforms.

### 6. Social adaptation

- Rebuild vertical layouts rather than cropping; the phone's lines break more often.
- The proposition must be readable inside three seconds, muted.
- Keep text and logo inside platform safe areas (clear of the top ~14% and bottom ~20% on 9:16).
- A cutdown of about 15 seconds: shorter ads earn slightly better recognition, and length does not buy attention.

### 7. Effectiveness

- Brand cue in the first second and present throughout; three or more distinct brand moments.
- Single-minded message.
- Must work silent.
- Specific call to action, visible long enough to act on.

## Critique of our own direction

**Strong.** It is built from assets the brand already owns (quill, name, tagline, its best line of copy). It shows breadth as one continuous act instead of a list. It is achievable at high finish with vector and type.

**Weak or cliché-prone, and the fix adopted for each.**

1. *Late branding.* A logo reveal at the end fails the research. **Fix:** the quill is on screen from frame one as the thing that draws, a notebook header carries the name throughout, and the wordmark closes the film.
2. *"Sketch turns into app" has been done.* **Fix:** the transformation is the single dramatic beat of the film, placed on a downbeat after a gap in the music, and it changes everything at once: line quality, colour world, type, sound.
3. *A line wandering between scenes can read as a screensaver.* **Fix:** in each scene the line is the subject and does work; scene changes are camera moves along it, with no hard cuts.
4. *Monochrome plus one accent is the default look of developer tools.* **Fix:** the warm paper world is where the film starts and ends; the dark "engine room" is reserved for automation only, as contrast; the accent is red chalk, which has a reason to exist.
5. *Abstraction can under-sell a small agency.* **Fix:** a proof beat with real shipped project screens and the site's own delivery timelines.

## Clichés to avoid

- Stock footage, people pointing at screens, handshake shots.
- A services list, an icon grid, or a row of technology logos.
- Vitruvian Man, parchment textures, gears and flying machines.
- Glowing network globes, particles forming a logo, lens flares.
- Bouncy overshoot on type, typewriter effects everywhere, glitch transitions.
- Fake dashboards with invented growth numbers.
- A narrator saying "In today's fast-paced digital world".
- Logo only in the last two seconds.
